There's a moment most students recognize. You're mid-technique, it's not quite working, and instead of adjusting, you just... push harder. Muscle through it. Force the finish.

It feels like effort. It feels like trying. But more often than not, it's actually the thing holding your jiu-jitsu back.

## What Forcing Actually Looks Like

Forcing doesn't always mean brute strength, though that's part of it. It can also look like:

- Committing to a sweep when your partner has already shifted their weight
- Chasing a wristlock long after the angle has closed
- Rushing through the setup because you want to get to the "good part"

In each case, you've stopped responding to what's actually in front of you and started fighting your own idea of what *should* be happening. That gap between idea and reality is where techniques fall apart.

## Why It Feels Right in the Moment

Forcing techniques is reinforced by adrenaline. When you're training at even moderate intensity, the pressure to *do something* is real. Your brain wants resolution. Committing harder feels decisive.

But jiu-jitsu — and this applies to the grappling work we do at Jitsu-Do Karate here in Newmarket — is fundamentally a responsive art. The technique exists to solve a specific problem created by a specific position. If that position shifts and you don't shift with it, you're no longer doing the technique. You're doing a reasonable impression of it while hoping gravity cooperates.

Shihan Ginn often puts it simply: if you have to fight the technique, it's not ready yet.

## What Happens to Your Body When You Force

There's a physical cost too. Forcing through resistance means your larger muscle groups are compensating for poor position. You fatigue faster. You accumulate tension in places that should stay relaxed — shoulders, jaw, forearms.

Over time, this trains bad habits into your nervous system. Your body learns to associate that technique with strain. Eventually the technique only works when you're fresh and your partner is cooperative, which is not a useful skill set.

Relaxation isn't a personality trait reserved for calm people. It's a technical requirement. A tense body gives slow, telegraphed movement. A relaxed body can feel shifts and respond to them before the conscious mind catches up.

## The Alternative: Reading Before Committing

This is harder to teach than it sounds, because it asks you to slow down mentally even when the pace feels fast.

Before you commit to a technique, ask: is the opening actually there, or am I creating it in my head? If you have to manufacture the entry by overpowering resistance, the position hasn't given it to you yet.

Practice pausing — genuinely pausing — when you feel a technique stalling. Don't abandon it immediately, but don't double down either. Feel where your partner's weight is. Notice what they're reacting to. Often the resistance itself tells you exactly where the next opening is.

This is why drilling matters so much. Not just drilling the technique, but drilling the *setup* — the moment before the moment. When you've run an entry hundreds of times, you start to recognize the texture of a real opening versus a forced one.

## Progress Looks Different Than You Expect

Students who stop forcing usually go through a frustrating middle period. They've given up muscling through things but haven't yet developed the sensitivity to catch openings cleanly. Techniques seem to fall apart more than before.

This is a good sign. It means the old habit is gone and the real skill is being built. Stick with it. The timing sharpens. The reads get faster. And eventually you're finishing techniques on people who are bigger and stronger than you — not because you overpowered them, but because you were patient enough to wait for the right moment and precise enough to use it.

That's the whole point.
