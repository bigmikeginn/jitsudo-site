Shisei means posture in Japanese. But in karate, it means something closer to bearing — the way you hold yourself when nobody is watching, when you're tired, when the drill is boring, when you'd rather be anywhere else.

That's where the real training is.

## It Starts With the Body

When Shihan Ginn corrects a student's posture, he's not being picky about aesthetics. A dropped chin, rounded shoulders, or a collapsed stance isn't just inefficient — it's a signal. It tells your nervous system that you're not ready. It tells an opponent the same thing.

Good shisei means chin level, shoulders back and down, spine long, weight distributed and grounded. Feet connected to the floor. Eyes forward. It's a simple description and a surprisingly hard thing to maintain through an entire class.

Your body will drift. That's normal. The practice is noticing when it does and correcting it — again and again, without frustration.

## The Mind Follows the Body

Here's something most people figure out after a few months of consistent training: how you stand affects how you think.

Slump in your chair long enough and your mood follows. Stand tall — not stiffly, but with intention — and something shifts internally. This isn't a motivational speech. It's something you can test yourself in the next ten minutes.

In karate, we use the body as the entry point because it's concrete and immediate. You can't directly instruct your confidence to improve. But you can fix your posture right now. You can take a breath, reset your stance, and try again. The mental state tends to follow.

Students who train at our dojo in Newmarket often say the most surprising change they notice isn't a technique or a belt — it's that they started walking differently. Standing differently in line at the grocery store. Sitting differently at their desk. Nobody told them to. It just happened, because they'd been practicing it for an hour several times a week.

## Shisei Under Pressure

The real test of posture isn't during a relaxed warm-up. It's when you're gassed, three rounds into sparring, or halfway through a grading with your legs burning.

That's when everything wants to collapse inward — shoulders curl, head drops, breathing gets shallow. And that's exactly when Shihan Ginn will remind you to stand up.

Not because it looks better. Because it works better. An upright spine breathes better. A grounded stance recovers faster. And a student who can hold their composure physically when they're under stress is building something that carries well beyond the dojo floor.

This is the point of karate that doesn't make it into the promotional material. Nobody puts "you'll learn to stand up straight when things get hard" on a flyer. But that's one of the most practical things the training gives you.

## A Daily Practice, Not a Class Technique

Shisei isn't a move you drill on Tuesdays. It's a standard you hold yourself to every day, in every context.

How do you sit when you're watching TV? How do you stand when you're waiting? How do you carry yourself when you're frustrated, or rushed, or self-conscious? These aren't karate questions — they're life questions. But karate gives you a framework for paying attention to them.

The dojo is a controlled environment where the feedback is immediate. Shihan will see the slouch. Your training partner will feel the weak stance when they push on it. That honest feedback loop, repeated consistently, builds a new default.

And over time, the new default starts showing up outside the dojo too.

## Start Where You Are

If you've never thought much about posture — if you spend most of your day at a desk or in a car — you might be surprised how much you're already compensating. Tight hips, stiff thoracic spine, forward head. These aren't character flaws. They're just patterns, and patterns can change.

Karate won't fix everything overnight. But it will give you something to practice and someone to practice it with, in a space where it actually matters.
