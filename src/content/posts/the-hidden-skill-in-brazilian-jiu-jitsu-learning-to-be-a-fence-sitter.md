Most people come to grappling arts looking for moves. They want submissions, escapes, sweeps. Techniques they can name and practise until they work.

That is completely reasonable. Techniques matter.

But after a while on the mat, you start to notice something. The people who improve fastest are not always the ones who know the most moves. They are the ones who can sit still inside a bad position without panicking.

That skill has a name in sport science and psychology: **tolerance for ambiguity**. On the mat, we call it being a fence sitter.

## What "Fence Sitting" Actually Means

In BJJ, you are almost never in a purely winning or purely losing position. You are usually somewhere in between — half guard, a scramble, a grip battle where neither person has the upper hand yet.

A fence sitter does not force the issue. They read the moment, wait for information, and move when the opening is real rather than imagined.

This sounds passive. It is not. It takes enormous discipline to stay relaxed and observant when someone heavier than you is trying to flatten you to the floor. The instinct is to explode, to grab at whatever is nearby, to do *something*. Fence sitting is the practice of overriding that instinct long enough to see what is actually there.

## Why Most People Skip This Skill

Because it does not look impressive. You cannot post a video of yourself calmly breathing through side control and rack up views. There is no highlight reel for patience.

New students especially want to move. Stillness feels like losing. So they burn energy fighting positions that have not fully developed, miss the transition that was coming two seconds later, and wonder why their technique keeps failing against more experienced partners.

The experienced partner was not necessarily stronger or faster. They were waiting. They had already decided not to react until the right moment arrived.

## How You Build It

The honest answer is: slowly, through repetition, and mostly by failing a lot first.

Positional sparring helps more than most people expect. When you set a timer and spend three minutes doing nothing but surviving from bottom half guard, you stop treating discomfort as an emergency. You learn that you can breathe, think, and function under pressure — that the position is not as desperate as it feels.

That feedback loop changes how your nervous system responds. Over time, the alarm bells get quieter. You start noticing the small shifts in weight and grip that signal an opportunity.

Coaching matters here too. A good instructor will stop you mid-roll not because you made a technical error, but because you rushed a decision that did not need to be rushed. That kind of feedback is harder to quantify than correcting a bad armbar grip, but it is just as valuable.

## The Crossover Into Real Life

This is not a stretch — it is something practitioners genuinely notice.

Learning to sit with uncertainty on the mat tends to make you a little calmer in situations off the mat where the outcome is not yet clear. A difficult conversation at work. A problem without an obvious solution. A moment where doing nothing is genuinely the right call.

You are not suddenly enlightened. You are just a bit less reactive, because you have practised being less reactive several times a week for months or years.

Here in Newmarket, we see this shift in students across age groups — adults who started training for fitness and found themselves better at handling pressure in their regular lives. That is not marketing language. It is just what consistent mat time tends to produce.

## The Move Underneath the Moves

Every technique in BJJ assumes you can stay composed long enough to apply it. The fence-sitting skill is what makes every other skill possible.

When you stop treating every bad position as a five-alarm situation, you create space to actually think. And when you can think, you can use what you know.

That is the hidden skill. Not flashy. Not easy to teach in a single class. But once you start developing it, everything else on the mat tends to get a little cleaner.
