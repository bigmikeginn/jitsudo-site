Walk into any Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu class at Jitsu-Do and the first ten minutes look almost too simple. People shrimping across the mat. Bridging. Rolling forward and back. Standing up from the ground and sitting back down again, over and over.

If you're new, you might wonder when the "real" training starts. Here's the truth: it already has. Those warm-up drills aren't filler before the lesson. In many ways, they *are* the lesson — and they're doing far more for you than you think.

### The Movements Are the Techniques

Every core movement in BJJ shows up first as a warm-up drill.

The shrimp — or hip escape — is the single most important movement in jiu-jitsu. It's how you create space when someone is on top of you, how you escape side control, how you recover guard. You will shrimp hundreds of times in warm-ups before you ever need it in a live roll. And when that moment comes, your body already knows the way out.

The bridge is the same story. It's the foundation of escaping the mount, the most uncomfortable position in grappling. A good bridge isn't about strength. It's about timing, hip drive, and knowing where your weight needs to go. You practice it when nobody is on top of you so that it's there when somebody is.

The technical stand-up might be the most practical of all. It's how you get up off the ground safely while keeping yourself protected — a skill that matters far beyond the mats. Every time you drill it, you're rehearsing the safest way to get back to your feet.

So the warm-up isn't separate from the techniques. It's the techniques, stripped down to their simplest form, repeated until they live in your body instead of just in your head.

### It Warms Up More Than Your Muscles

Yes, the obvious part matters too. BJJ asks a lot of your body — your hips, shoulders, neck, and spine all move in ways they don't in everyday life. Going from sitting at a desk all day to live grappling with no preparation is how people get hurt.

A good warm-up raises your temperature, loosens your joints, and wakes up the muscles that protect them. Your neck gets moving before anyone puts pressure on it. Your hips open up before you ask them to shrimp and bridge. Your shoulders get warm before they carry your weight in a roll.

This matters even more for our adult students. A lot of the people training BJJ in Newmarket are in their thirties, forties, and beyond. They're not twenty-year-old athletes, and they don't need to train like one. A proper warm-up is what lets them train hard, week after week, year after year, without breaking down. It's not the cautious option. It's the smart one.

### It Trains Your Brain, Not Just Your Body

Here's the part most people miss: warm-up drills are a coordination workout.

Moving on the ground is unnatural at first. Most adults haven't rolled, crawled, or shrimped since they were kids. The warm-up is where your nervous system relearns how to move in all directions — forward, backward, sideways, upside down — without having to think about it.

That's why beginners who take the warm-up seriously progress faster. They're not just getting fit. They're building the movement vocabulary that everything else in BJJ is written in. A student who can shrimp smoothly, bridge with timing, and roll without hesitation is a student who can actually learn the techniques that follow — because their body isn't fighting the movement anymore.

Watch two white belts in their first live roll. The one who drilled the warm-up moves like they belong on the ground. The one who skipped them looks like they're wrestling a mattress. Same techniques taught, completely different result.

### The First Ten Minutes Set the Tone

There's a discipline to it, too. The warm-up is where you show up and get to work, no excuses, no easing in. You don't wait until you feel like training. You start moving, and the feeling follows.

That habit carries into everything else. Students who take the first ten minutes seriously tend to take the whole class seriously. They listen better, drill with more intent, and roll with more control. The warm-up is where the mindset for the rest of the session gets built.

Kids learn this fastest of all. They don't overthink the drills — they just do them, and within weeks they're moving in ways that surprise their parents. The structure of the warm-up gives them a routine they can count on, and the repetition gives them confidence they can feel.

### What a Good Warm-Up Looks Like

At Jitsu-Do, the warm-up is never random. It's built around the movements you'll actually need:

- **Shrimping and bridging** — the escapes, practiced until they're automatic.
- **Forward and backward rolls** — learning to move and fall safely in every direction.
- **Technical stand-ups** — getting to your feet without exposing yourself.
- **Animal movements and hip mobility** — opening up the hips and shoulders so everything else works better.

Ten to fifteen minutes, every class, no shortcuts. By the time we get to technique, your body is warm, your brain is switched on, and the movements already feel familiar — because you've been doing them since the minute you stepped on the mat.

### Don't Skip the Boring Part

Every experienced grappler will tell you the same thing: the "boring" basics are what save you. Nobody ever lost a roll because their warm-up was too thorough. Plenty of people have lost one because they never learned to shrimp properly.

So next time you're tempted to stroll in late and skip the drills, remember what's actually happening in those first ten minutes. You're not warming up *for* jiu-jitsu. You're doing jiu-jitsu — the most fundamental version of it there is.

And that foundation is what everything else is built on.

### **FAQ: BJJ Warm-Up Drills**

**Do I need to be fit before I start BJJ?**
No. The warm-up meets you where you are. The drills are scalable — you move at your own pace, and your fitness builds as you train. Most of our new students are surprised how quickly their bodies adapt, usually within the first few weeks.

**What if I can't do the drills — like shrimping or rolling?**
Then you're exactly who they're for. Nobody walks in knowing how to move on the ground. The instructors break each movement down, and you'll get better at them every single class. Awkward at first is completely normal.

**How long is the warm-up?**
Usually ten to fifteen minutes at the start of every class. It's the same structure each time, so you always know what's coming — and you can measure your own progress by how much easier it feels month to month.

**Do kids do the same warm-up?**
The same movements, adjusted for age and size. Kids actually take to the drills faster than adults most of the time — rolling and crawling still feels like play to them, and that playfulness is exactly what makes them good at it.
