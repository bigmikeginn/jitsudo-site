There is an old concept in karate called *metsuke* — sometimes translated as "the eyes of a warrior," sometimes simply as "gaze." It sounds poetic, but the practical idea behind it is straightforward: where your attention goes, your body follows. Where you fix your focus, your mind gets anchored. The two are not separate things.

Most beginners do not think about this at all. They look at their own hands during kata. They stare at an opponent's fist when sparring. They glance at the floor when they lose balance. None of this is wrong, exactly — it is just unaware. And awareness is the whole point.

## What Metsuke Actually Means in Practice

Traditional metsuke describes a kind of soft, wide focus — looking at a person without locking onto any single point. Not staring at the shoulder, not watching the eyes too hard, not tracking the hands. Instead, you take in the whole picture. Your gaze rests somewhere around the centre of the other person, and your peripheral vision does the real work.

This is harder than it sounds. The natural instinct under pressure is to narrow your focus — to pick a point and tunnel in. That instinct will get you hit. The moment you fix on one hand, the other hand becomes invisible. The moment you watch the face, you miss the foot.

In class, we work on this constantly, often without naming it directly. When Shihan Ginn tells a student to "stay relaxed and open," part of what he means is this: do not collapse your awareness down to a pinhole. Keep the wide view.

## It Goes Beyond Sparring

Metsuke is not just a sparring tool. It shows up everywhere in training.

In kata, your eyes should move with intention and purpose — not drifting, not darting, not blank. Each turn has a direction. Each technique has a corresponding look. Performing a kata with unfocused eyes is like playing a piece of music while staring at your fingers. Technically functional, but something important is missing.

In partner drills, your gaze communicates. Confidence or hesitation both show up in the eyes before they show up in the body. Students learn, sometimes uncomfortably, how much they reveal without meaning to.

And there is a mental dimension here too. Where you put your attention shapes your experience. If you spend a drill mentally replaying the mistake you just made, your focus is in the past. If you are already worried about the next technique, your focus is in the future. Neither place is where you are standing. Metsuke, in that sense, is also about being present — actually here, in this moment, in this room.

## Training the Gaze

You can work on this intentionally. A few things that help:

**Slow down.** When you perform kata at a pace that gives you time to think, you can practice placing your eyes deliberately with each movement. Speed comes later. Awareness comes first.

**Use a mirror carefully.** Mirrors are useful, but students sometimes get too attached to watching themselves. Occasionally, train without looking at your reflection. Feel what is happening instead of watching it.

**Notice the habit.** Simply pay attention to where your eyes go under pressure. Do you look down when you are uncertain? Do you look away when corrected? Noticing the habit is the first step to changing it.

**Stay curious, not critical.** A hard, self-critical stare turned inward is just as limiting as a stare fixed on one point of an opponent. Soft awareness works better than rigid judgment, in practice and in life.

## Why This Matters Outside the Dojo

Here in Newmarket, life moves fast. People walk around with their eyes on their phones, their attention fragmented across a dozen things at once. Training metsuke is, in a quiet way, a push back against that. It is practice in choosing where your attention goes, rather than letting it be pulled around by whatever is loudest.

That is not a small thing. Focused, calm, wide-open awareness — the kind you build slowly through repetition on the dojo floor — tends to carry over. Students notice it themselves. Situations that used to feel rushed start to feel more readable. People become easier to understand. Decisions feel less reactive.

Where you look is where you are. Train the gaze, and you train the mind.
