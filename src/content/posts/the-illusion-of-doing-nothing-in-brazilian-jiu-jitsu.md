Watch two experienced grapplers roll and you might wonder if anything is happening at all. They look calm. Almost motionless. Compare that to two beginners, who thrash and scramble and gasp for air, and you'd be forgiven for thinking the beginners are working harder.

They are working harder. That's the problem.

## Still on the Outside, Busy on the Inside

What looks like doing nothing in Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu is actually a constant, quiet conversation between two bodies. Weight is shifting by a few centimetres. Grips are being tested without committing. Pressure is applied, released, redirected. The experienced practitioner isn't waiting — they're reading.

They're feeling for tension in the other person's frame. They're noticing where weight is loaded and where it isn't. They're setting up positions the way a chess player sets up a board — three moves before the move that matters.

None of that shows up on video. Almost none of it shows up in real time to a casual observer. But it's the whole game.

## Why Beginners Burn Out Fast

New students often leave their first few classes exhausted in a way that doesn't make sense to them. They weren't on the mat that long. But they used every muscle they had, at full effort, the entire time — because they didn't yet have the tools to be efficient.

When you don't know what to look for, you respond to everything. Every shift feels like a threat. Every grip feels like something you need to break immediately. You're reacting instead of thinking, and reaction at full speed is expensive.

This is completely normal. It's also temporary, if you keep showing up.

Over time, you start to recognize patterns. You stop panicking when someone takes your back because you've been there before and you know there's a process. You stop wasting energy on grips that don't matter because you've learned which ones do. The chaos starts to have shape.

## What "Relaxing" Actually Means

Instructors tell beginners to relax all the time, and beginners almost never know what that means in practice. It sounds like advice to care less. It isn't.

Relaxing in grappling means using the right amount of tension for what's needed right now — not more. It means your lats aren't firing when your lats don't need to fire. It means you're breathing. It means your attention is on information, not survival.

A relaxed grappler feels heavy in a way that has nothing to do with their actual weight. They're connected to the mat, connected to their partner, and they're not giving away anything through unnecessary movement or bracing.

That quality takes years to develop. You can't fake it and you can't rush it. But you can work toward it from your very first class.

## The Patience Problem

Here in Newmarket, like anywhere else, people come to martial arts with different expectations. Some want to move fast and feel powerful right away. There's nothing wrong with that energy — it gets people in the door.

But BJJ has a way of humbling that impulse, and that's part of what makes it valuable. The art rewards patience in a very direct, physical way. Impatience costs you position. Rushing costs you control. The person who waits one beat longer — who resists the urge to move before the moment is right — usually wins the exchange.

That's a lesson that doesn't stay on the mat. Most people who train long enough start to notice it showing up elsewhere.

## The Beginner's Advantage

Here's something worth saying directly: the illusion of doing nothing is actually harder to see through when you're new, and that's okay. You're supposed to be lost at first. You're building a reference library from scratch.

Every roll where you get caught, every position that feels impossible, every moment where you can't figure out what the other person just did — that's data. You're accumulating the experience that will eventually let you feel what's happening before it happens.

The stillness you see in experienced grapplers isn't the absence of skill. It's skill so internalized that it no longer needs to announce itself.
