## Speed Is Not the Point

When people first watch a jiu-jitsu class, they usually focus on the fast parts — the quick throw, the sudden joint lock, the moment everything seems to happen at once. It looks impressive. But if you asked Shihan Ginn what actually builds skill, he would point you to the moments that are almost boring to watch.

At Jitsu-Do Karate in Newmarket, we talk about three distinct speeds of training, and understanding them changes how you practise.

## Speed One: Slow and Deliberate

This is where technique is actually learned.

When you move slowly through a wrist lock or a hip throw, your brain has time to process what your body is doing. You feel where your weight is. You notice when your elbow drifts out of position. You understand *why* the technique works — the angle, the leverage, the timing relative to your partner's balance.

Slowly is not the same as weakly. You are still applying real pressure, real structure, real intent. You are just giving yourself the gift of awareness.

Most beginners want to skip this speed. They feel self-conscious moving slowly, like it looks like they do not know what they are doing. The truth is the opposite — moving slowly with precision is one of the clearest signs that someone understands their art.

## Speed Two: Moderate Flow

Once a technique starts to feel natural at slow speed, you bring it up to what we call flow pace. This is a conversational tempo — not rushed, not crawling. You and your partner are moving together in a way that starts to resemble real application.

At this speed, transitions matter. You are not just practising single techniques in isolation anymore. You are learning how one movement connects to the next. If your first attempt at a throw does not complete, what do you do? Flow pace is where you start to figure that out without the pressure of full resistance.

This is also where a lot of the teaching happens on the floor. Shihan can watch you move at this speed and see clearly whether your base is sound, whether you are muscling the technique, whether your partner is compensating for something you are not doing correctly.

## Speed Three: Realistic Pressure

Eventually you have to know whether what you have been practising actually works when someone is not cooperating perfectly. That is what the third speed is for.

This does not mean going reckless. It means applying the technique with real intent against a partner who is offering genuine resistance — controlled, respectful resistance, but resistance nonetheless. You find out quickly what was held together by your partner's compliance and what was held together by actual technique.

This speed is humbling. It is supposed to be. The point is not to "win" the drill. The point is to find the gaps in your understanding so you can go back to speed one and fix them.

## Why All Three Matter Together

The mistake is training at only one speed.

If you only train slowly, you build precision but never test it. Your technique looks clean in practice but has never been stress-tested.

If you only train fast and hard, you tend to fill in the gaps with strength and momentum rather than skill. You get by in the short term, but you build habits that eventually hit a ceiling — and that ceiling arrives faster as you get older.

The three speeds work as a cycle. You drill slowly to build understanding, move to flow pace to build connection between techniques, and test at realistic speed to reveal what still needs work. Then you go back to slow and refine.

This cycle is why jiu-jitsu, done properly, is something you can keep training for decades. You are always learning something, regardless of what level you are at.

## It Takes Patience

Nobody walks into their first class and immediately gets comfortable with all three speeds. That is fine. Part of what a good instructor does is read where a student is and push them into the right speed at the right time — not so comfortable that nothing is being challenged, not so overwhelmed that nothing is being learned.
