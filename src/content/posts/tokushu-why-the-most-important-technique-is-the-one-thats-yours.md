There's a concept in traditional karate called *tokushu* — a special or personal technique. It's not something you're assigned. It's something that finds you over time, through honest, repetitive training.

Not every technique fits every body. A short, powerful fighter may find that a low side kick is devastating for them. A longer-limbed practitioner might discover that a snapping front kick creates distance before an opponent can close the gap. Someone with exceptional hip rotation might develop a reverse punch that lands with a consistency and power others just can't replicate.

This isn't about ignoring the rest of your curriculum. It's about understanding yourself.

## The Difference Between Learning and Owning

In the early stages of training, you learn everything broadly. You drill stances, blocks, strikes, and combinations. You practice katas you don't fully understand yet. That's the point — you're building a vocabulary.

But somewhere along the way, something clicks. You throw a technique and it feels different. Cleaner. More natural. You land it in sparring without thinking. Your instructor notices. You start to trust it.

That's the beginning of a tokushu.

Owning a technique means you've moved past performing it correctly and into expressing it instinctively. There's a big gap between those two things, and it usually takes years to cross.

## Why This Matters More Than You Think

In a real self-defense situation — which is ultimately what traditional karate prepares you for — you don't get to browse your options. You respond with what's most available to you under stress. That's almost always your strongest, most-practiced technique.

Training everything equally sounds disciplined, but in practice it often means nothing is deeply reliable. A practitioner who has thrown a particular elbow strike ten thousand times is going to access that tool under pressure far more readily than someone who has thrown fifty different techniques two hundred times each.

Shihan Ginn often returns to this idea in class: depth matters more than breadth once you've built a solid foundation. Knowing your go-to technique — and trusting it — is part of building that depth.

## How You Find It

You don't really choose your tokushu. You discover it.

Pay attention during sparring to what actually lands. Not what you want to work, not what looks impressive — what connects, what creates openings, what you reach for without planning to.

Pay attention during kata to what feels alive versus what feels mechanical. Sometimes a single movement in a form will stop you mid-practice because it suddenly makes sense in a way it didn't before.

Ask your instructor. Shihan Ginn and the senior students at the dojo notice patterns in your movement that you can't see yourself. A good coach will often recognize your strongest technique before you do.

And then — once you've identified it — go deeper. Drill it from different angles. Drill it when you're tired. Drill it against different partners. Find every variation. Understand what sets it up and what it sets up in return.

## It Still Lives Inside the Tradition

Developing a tokushu doesn't mean drifting away from traditional karate. It means you're growing within it.

The kata we practice at Jitsu-Do here in Newmarket contain hundreds of techniques, but masters throughout history were known for specific ones. They didn't skip the rest of their training — they understood the whole system and then went exceptionally deep on what suited them. That's the model.

Your personal technique should be the sharpest tool in a full toolbox, not a shortcut around learning the craft.

## Give Yourself Permission to Specialize

A lot of students feel like focusing on one technique is somehow cheating — like they should be equally competent at everything at all times. That thinking keeps a lot of people stuck at a surface level for longer than necessary.

You are not a catalog of techniques. You are a person with a particular body, particular instincts, and a particular way of moving. The art should fit you, not the other way around.

Find what works. Build it seriously. Let it become yours.
