There's a moment in early karate training when a student realizes that meeting force with force is exhausting. You can only absorb so many hard blocks before your forearms are bruised and your confidence starts to crack. That's usually when a good instructor introduces the concept of *nagashi*.

Nagashi is a Japanese term that translates roughly as "flowing" or "washing away." In practice, it means redirecting an incoming attack rather than stopping it dead. Instead of planting yourself in the path of a strike and muscling through it, you angle your body or your limb just enough to guide the force past you — and you use that moment to counter.

It sounds simple. Like most things in karate, it takes a while to actually feel it.

## The Physics Are on Your Side

Think about what happens when you block a punch by driving your arm straight into it. You're pitting your strength against your attacker's strength at the point of impact. If they're bigger or faster, you're at a disadvantage before the exchange even starts.

Now think about redirecting that same punch. Instead of stopping it, you make light contact and guide it slightly off its line — just a few degrees is often enough. The attacker's momentum carries them forward and slightly off balance. You haven't matched their force. You've used it against them.

This is why nagashi matters for people of every size and age. You don't have to be the strongest person in the room to make it work. You have to be precise and calm.

## How We Train It at Jitsu-Do

At the dojo in Newmarket, we work nagashi principles into drilling from a relatively early stage — often through partner work where one person attacks with a controlled straight punch and the other practices the receiving hand softening, angling, and deflecting rather than blocking hard.

At first, students almost always tense up. The instinct when something is coming at your face is to tighten everything and push back. Nagashi asks you to do the opposite: stay relaxed, read the line of the attack, and move with it just enough.

Shihan Ginn often uses a simple image in class — water doesn't stop a rock by meeting it head-on. It flows around it. The rock still gets where it's going, but the water is fine.

Once students start feeling it in drills, it shows up in their kata too. Many of the receiving movements in traditional kata aren't hard blocks at all. They're deflections. When you understand nagashi, whole sequences of movements that seemed puzzling start to make sense.

## It Changes How You Move Overall

One of the quieter benefits of training this principle is what it does to your body overall. Hard blocking builds tension. Nagashi-based movement builds sensitivity — the ability to feel where force is going and respond to it rather than fight it.

That sensitivity carries over into sparring, into self-defense scenarios, and honestly into the way you carry yourself. Students who work nagashi seriously tend to get lighter on their feet. They stop bracing so much. They start reading their training partners better because they're paying attention to incoming energy rather than just waiting for their turn to hit.

It also pairs naturally with footwork. A small step offline combined with a redirecting hand can completely change the geometry of an exchange. You're no longer standing in front of a problem. You've stepped to the side of it.

## Strength Matters — But Timing Matters More

None of this means strength is useless. A well-conditioned body is still a real asset in any physical confrontation. But strength without timing and sensitivity leaves gaps. A smaller person with good nagashi timing can manage a much bigger opponent because they're not trying to overpower anyone — they're using what's already coming at them.

This is one of the reasons traditional martial arts have stayed relevant for centuries. The techniques weren't built for athletes in their prime. They were built to be functional for ordinary people under real pressure. Nagashi is part of that practical foundation.
